# Energy and climate: AI will only intensify climate change.

## A. The claim

**The headline claim:** “AI will only intensify climate change.”

This is the headline of [Juan Cole’s December 2025 article in The Nation](https://www.thenation.com/article/environment/ai-climate-change-bill-gates/).

**The careful version we also checked:** “AI’s growing electricity demand could increase US carbon emissions by 2030.”

Here, “increase” means more emissions than there would be without AI’s extra demand; total US emissions could still go down. This narrows [a warning from Cornell researchers](https://news.cornell.edu/stories/2025/11/roadmap-shows-environmental-impact-ai-data-center-boom) that computing demand is growing faster than electricity is getting cleaner.

## B. The short answer

Too certain. AI is pushing up demand for electricity, and where gas or coal plants supply it, emissions are higher than they’d be without AI’s extra demand. But how much depends on how fast demand grows and how clean the grid gets, and the [International Energy Agency](https://www.iea.org/reports/key-questions-on-energy-and-ai/executive-summary) also sees new clean power being built. Possible climate benefits from AI are [unproven, not ruled out](https://www.iea.org/reports/energy-and-ai/ai-and-climate-change).

## C. What does this claim actually mean?

Building AI models and answering questions takes a lot of computing, plus cooling. AI is only part of what data centers do; they also run everyday online services. [Berkeley Lab’s report](https://eta-publications.lbl.gov/sites/default/files/2024-12/lbnl-2024-united-states-data-center-energy-usage-report.pdf?stream=top) estimates these different uses.

Using electricity and causing carbon emissions aren’t the same thing. The same amount of power can be clean or dirty depending on the power plants that make it. And a company that buys enough renewable power over a year may still run on fossil power at some hours or in some places. [IEA, 2026, page 46](https://iea.blob.core.windows.net/assets/3179f7f8-01f6-4dd6-bffa-c9f7b73f1dc9/KeyQuestionsonEnergyandAI.pdf).

This page is about electricity and climate. Our [water page](/claims/water) covers cooling and local water.

## D. What evidence do we actually have?

**The US so far:** Berkeley Lab estimated that all US data centers used 4.4% of the country’s electricity in 2023. That’s an estimate for all data centers, not just AI, and it has gaps. [Report, pages 6 and 67–68](https://eta-publications.lbl.gov/sites/default/files/2024-12/lbnl-2024-united-states-data-center-energy-usage-report.pdf?stream=top).

**Worldwide forecast:** The International Energy Agency (IEA) estimates data centers worldwide used 485 terawatt-hours of electricity in 2025 and expects about 950 by 2030, roughly double. That includes non-AI work. It also found US companies planning gas power plants on site, though not every announced project gets built. [2026 report](https://www.iea.org/reports/key-questions-on-energy-and-ai/executive-summary).

**One state’s warning:** Virginia’s state audit warned in December 2024 that fast data-center growth could make clean-energy goals harder and keep fossil plants open longer. That’s one state’s forecast, not a national certainty. [Audit, pages 30 and 88](https://rga.lis.virginia.gov/Published/2025/RD206/PDF).

**Possible upsides:** Computers keep getting more efficient, and AI could help manage energy better. But those savings depend on people actually using them, and more use can cancel them out. Nobody has shown they add up to a net cut. [IEA efficiency analysis](https://www.iea.org/reports/key-questions-on-energy-and-ai/executive-summary); [IEA climate assessment](https://www.iea.org/reports/energy-and-ai/ai-and-climate-change).

## E. Hype score and explanation

**Hype score for the headline: 4/5 — Overheated.** “Will” ignores the uncertainty the [IEA](https://www.iea.org/reports/key-questions-on-energy-and-ai/executive-summary) describes, and “only” rules out climate benefits that are [unproven but possible](https://www.iea.org/reports/energy-and-ai/ai-and-climate-change). A claim that overstates on both counts scores at least 4.

**The careful version scores 1/5 — Grounded.** It compares emissions with a US without AI’s extra demand, and it says “could”. That matches the growing demand and the real chance it’s met with gas power, as the [IEA](https://www.iea.org/reports/key-questions-on-energy-and-ai/executive-summary) describes. A low hype score doesn’t mean low harm to the climate.

## F. What can you do if you are still concerned?

- **See how your region makes its electricity.** [EPA’s Power Profiler](https://www.epa.gov/egrid/power-profiler) shows your area’s power mix, with a [help page](https://www.epa.gov/egrid/frequent-questions-about-egrid). It can’t work out the footprint of your own chatbot use.
- **Comment on a utility plan.** Find your [state utility commission](https://www.naruc.org/about-naruc/our-mission/state-associate-and-federal-members/) and its [guide to taking part](https://www.naruc.org/serving-the-public-interest/how-commissions-work/frequently-asked-questions/). Ask for the case number, then ask about expected demand, power sources, emissions and who pays. Each state works differently.
- **Get help with federal energy decisions.** [FERC’s Office of Public Participation](https://www.ferc.gov/OPP) helps you find filings and comment deadlines. It covers federal matters, not every local data center, and taking part doesn’t guarantee the outcome.
