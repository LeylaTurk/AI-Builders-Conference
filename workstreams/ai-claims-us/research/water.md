## Claim selection, provenance, and boundaries

**Exact claim being scored:** “AI data-center growth threatens local water supplies in water-stressed U.S. communities.”

This is a transparent editorial narrowing to the local risk proposition in [Bloomberg, May 8, 2025](https://www.bloomberg.com/graphics/2025-ai-impacts-data-centers-water-data/). The inspected article says, **“data centers threaten local water supplies”** in its paragraph immediately before the Newsha Ajami quotation. It discusses U.S. locations as well as overseas examples. This brief scores the U.S., site-specific risk proposition, not the headline's stronger implication of demonstrated widespread depletion. Its time scope is current and planned development, without a fixed deadline. “Threatens” means a credible risk requiring location-specific assessment; it does not assert that every water-stressed community already has a shortage caused by AI.

The starting reader question is broader than this claim. Distinct propositions not scored here are: AI is exhausting America's total water supply; every prompt consumes a bottle of water; water use is harmless because evaporation eventually returns as rain; and all data centers use the same cooling system. We explain the relevant distinctions without rating those different assertions together. No prevalence measurement supports calling this the most common water claim.

## Assessment

The claim fits an evidenced mechanism: added evaporative cooling demand competes with finite local supply and utility capacity, particularly during hot periods. Stronger statements of actual household deprivation need a specific utility, source, time period, competing demand, and causal evidence. Geographic water stress is a screening indicator, not proof of an individual project's harm. The public article establishes that the proposition occurs in discussion; the technical case rests on inspected laboratory research, utility-record analysis, and clearly labeled models.

National estimates concern data centers collectively. AI is an important source of additional computing demand, but workload attribution is not established by simply relabeling those totals. The research does not infer a town's spare water capacity from a U.S. annual number. Nor does it infer that a local problem is insignificant because a national percentage is small.

This is not a controlled experiment literature. JLARC supplies administrative observations; the national and per-query numbers are modeled estimates; the 2026 peak-capacity work is a preprint with scenarios. No expert probability survey or extinction-style hypothetical is used. Company reports appear as inputs to modeling and are not treated as independent audited measurements of AI-specific use.

## Interpretation safeguards

- **Direct versus indirect:** report the two separately. The electricity-related burden can occur in a different watershed. Reservoir allocation conventions matter when comparing estimates; they do not establish water that would have been saved by avoiding one prompt.
- **Local/seasonal:** request source-specific and monthly/maximum-day information. Annual efficiency cannot reveal summer capacity needs. Climate, cooling method, and power mix are material inputs, as W06–W08 show.
- **Water source:** potable, other freshwater, and reclaimed wastewater are different. Reuse can relieve drinking-water demand without proving zero environmental impact. JLARC discusses practical limits on availability and infrastructure in printed p. 65.
- **Per-query boundary:** W07 models operational consumption, not all manufacturing water or a universal present-day product. The same paper includes a distinct global 2027 withdrawal forecast; this brief does not conflate that forecast with U.S. consumption. A workload average is not a marginal household water saving.
- **Units:** liters and U.S. gallons are different units; 66 billion liters divided by 3.785411784 gives approximately 17.4 billion U.S. gallons. Website rounds to about 17 billion. No percent comparison mixes withdrawals and consumption.
- **Time:** older observed records remain labeled 2023. The March 2026 work brings forward analysis of peak capacity, not measurements of every 2026 facility. Projected quantities do not become observed merely because the paper is recent.
- **Independence:** Li and Han share authors, and newer papers reuse Berkeley Lab estimates. Do not count these as three independent national water censuses. JLARC's utility records provide a distinct empirical source.

## Practical-action boundaries

The website gives three linked actions: identify/contact the supplier, inspect local drought conditions, and participate in relevant discharge permitting. Requests for peak use, drought restrictions, cooling source and infrastructure costs are editorial suggestions, not statements that every utility must disclose them or has a pending proposal. Community-water reports are not available for every water supply arrangement. NPDES participation cannot substitute for a state's water-rights, withdrawal, municipal-service or zoning decisions; the website explicitly tells readers to ask which process governs allocation. No implication that personal chatbot avoidance eliminates a utility-scale risk is made.
