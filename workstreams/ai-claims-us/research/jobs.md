## Claim selection and boundaries

**The claim:** AI could eliminate half of US entry-level white-collar jobs within one to five years.

Faithful paraphrase of the May 28, 2025 Axios interview with Anthropic CEO Dario Amodei. His January 2026 first-person essay repeats the conditional displacement claim and explains its assumptions. “Could” is preserved; it is not converted into “will.” The clock starts with the original 2025 claim (approximately 2026–2030), not the page's update date. The unemployment-rate prediction appearing alongside the original warning is a separate proposition and is not scored here. We also do not score all AI, every occupation, or a particular reader's prospects. “Entry-level” is not a consistent measured category across datasets; age bands are proxies, not synonyms.

The essay uses both disruption and displacement language. We assess the narrower elimination/displacement reading of the original warning, explicitly identified above. Reorganizing tasks alone does not satisfy it. This is an example of a recurring public claim, not a prevalence measurement. The claim's proponent leads a company selling the technology; this is relevant provenance, not a reason to reject his argument.

## Synthesis and distinctions

The best recent evidence is mixed in a way that can be coherent: granular data show weaker early-career entry into highly exposed work, while national indicators do not yet show clear economy-wide transformation. ADP's dashboard and Stanford's paper share underlying data and must not be counted twice. Census administrative work is independent, but measures industry-state exposure and a different age band. None identifies an individual reader's risk. Hiring, employment stock, separations, unemployment, wages, and quality of work are distinct outcomes.

The 19% Stanford measure is a relative shortfall against peers' growth through June, whereas September's 4.4% number is a one-year observed employment change through August in the high-exposure group. They have different denominators and time windows. The website uses the latter with its comparison and keeps the former here to prevent a misleading comparison with the 50% claim.

Exposure is not adoption; adoption is not necessarily substitution; productivity improvement does not mechanically fix headcount because demand and work organization can change. BLS's source discussion makes those limits explicit. Reported company layoffs may be relevant cases but do not by themselves distinguish AI effects from demand, restructuring, financing, or other causes. The brief does not use press-release layoff totals as proven AI displacement. Wages may change differently from hiring: Stanford studies base pay, while Census describes earnings growth, which are not identical measures. Gallup adds worker perceptions of task benefits and responsibilities; it does not establish net improvement in job quality.

The evidence is predominantly observational, with one national business survey and a worker survey. The focal forecast is expert judgment with a theoretical pathway. No controlled experiment in this source set establishes economy-wide replacement. We do not label descriptive payroll comparisons experimental or translate a benchmark result into an occupational forecast.
