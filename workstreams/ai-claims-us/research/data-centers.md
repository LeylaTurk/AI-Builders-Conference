## Claim selection, provenance, and boundaries

**Headline claim scored:** “Data centers will destroy our community.”

Leyla asked for a claim like “Data centers will destroy my community.” The closest real public occurrence is Gina Mangham, identified as “with Renew DeKalb,” quoted by [CBS News Atlanta (Nakell Williams, November 18, 2025, 11:14 PM EST)](https://www.cbsnews.com/atlanta/news/dekalb-county-residents-push-back-against-proposal-that-could-bring-massive-data-centers-to-their-neighborhoods), headline “DeKalb County residents push back against proposal that could bring massive data centers to their neighborhoods.” Re-opened October 8, 2026; the quotation **“I believe this will destroy our community.”** is present, as is “We don't want data centers in our community.” The article concerns a DeKalb County zoning text amendment for data centers and current “data campus proposals.” **Note:** the article does not state where Mangham lives; the page therefore says “of the group Renew DeKalb” and does not call her a resident. Our headline replaces “this” with “Data centers,” a light generalization from the same speaker's words in the same article.

Second occurrence: [West Virginia Watch (Caity Coyne, May 28, 2025)](https://westvirginiawatch.com/2025/05/28/it-will-destroy-this-place-tucker-county-residents-fight-for-future-against-proposed-data-center/) quotes Tucker County resident Nikki Forrester: “Honestly, I feel like if this happens, it will destroy this place.” Confirmed October 8, 2026.

**Careful claim scored:** “Large data centers can bring real local costs, such as constant noise near homes, pressure on regional power bills and costly state tax breaks for few permanent jobs, while some host communities also gain large local tax revenue.” This is our wording, based mainly on Virginia's JLARC audit with corroboration from other sources below.

**Boundaries.** Water use and climate emissions have their own pages (`water`, `energy-climate`) and are pointed to only briefly. This page covers power bills and who pays for the grid, noise, land and rezoning, tax breaks versus local tax revenue, permanent jobs, backup generators and air, and local opposition. Opposition counts measure public reaction, not harm. No national figure for farmland converted to data centers was found in a credible source, and none is claimed.

## Assessment

The evidence supports “can impose serious, uneven local costs,” not “will destroy.” The best-documented costs are: constant low-frequency noise affecting some neighbors' well-being (not hearing damage); regional upward pressure on capacity prices in PJM, which the independent market monitor attributes primarily to data-center load and which consumers ultimately pay; large state sales-tax exemptions; and few permanent jobs per site. On the other side, some host localities gain large property-tax revenue (Loudoun County, Virginia: 38% of General Fund revenue), Virginia's audit found data centers currently pay their full cost of service there, and regulators are creating new rate classes meant to keep costs with the largest users. Future household bill increases are modeled projections. Historical research found load growth lowered average prices through 2024, but not significantly for residential prices and with explicit doubt about the future. No inspected source documents a host community being destroyed, and none measures a data center's overall net effect on a whole community.

Most detailed official evidence is from Virginia, the largest US market, which may not represent smaller or rural sites such as those in DeKalb County, Georgia, or Tucker County, West Virginia.

## Interpretation safeguards

- **Forecast versus bill:** the $14–$37 figure is a JLARC projection for one utility's territory, not a measured bill change. PJM capacity costs are one part of a retail bill and vary by utility and state.
- **Mixed price evidence:** LBNL's historical finding (D06) and JLARC's current cost-of-service finding (D01) are counterpoints; the PJM monitor (D03) is evidence of upward pressure. All three are reported, none overrides the others.
- **Uneven revenue:** Loudoun's 38% is an extreme. JLARC's range for mature localities runs from under 1% to 31%. Tax revenue may be countywide while noise falls on near neighbors.
- **Jobs:** JLARC's 74,000 statewide includes indirect and construction jobs; the page uses only the per-site permanent figure and the construction peak.
- **Generators:** JLARC's observed-use finding and the UCR modeled preprint differ in kind; the page reports JLARC and calls the modeled maximum a worst case.
- **Opposition counts** describe public reaction, not proof of harm.

## Practical-action boundaries

The page gives four actions: find the local government and hearing dates (USAGov), ask about noise limits and exemptions (NBC example), ask the state utility commission who pays for grid upgrades (NARUC list, SCC GS-5 example), and weigh permanent jobs against tax breaks (Georgia audit example). It does not promise that any utility or locality must disclose particular information, and says that speaking up does not guarantee an outcome.
